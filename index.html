In this studio, we explore how architecture can respond to ecological and environmental conditions within complex urban contexts. The site is analyzed through its flora and fauna, using graphics and color mapping to represent species and environmental zones. Precedents are studied and reinterpreted with techniques like pixel sorting, generating abstract images that translate environmental data into visual form. These images guide the development of initial 3D-printed models, which are then refined into a fitness center that responds to both program and environment. The design incorporates sustainable strategies, including wind energy generation. Unreal Engine renders visualize the building in its context, highlighting architecture as part of a larger ecological system connecting design, energy, and landscape.

Visual Studies III

Original ↗
Semester
Spring 2026
Instructors
Jennifer Chen
Curime Batliner
Devyn Weiser

In this course, we focus on how architectural work is documented, curated, and presented through the format of a portfolio. As students, we learn to organize projects from different studios and courses into a cohesive body of work, thinking about the portfolio not just as a collection, but as a way to communicate our own design approach and ideas. A key part of the class is the “Capture” section, where we photograph our models using a robotic arm in the Robot House. By adjusting lighting, backgrounds, and camera angles, we produce a large series of images that reveal different views, materials, and colors of each model, helping us understand how representation can shape perception. Alongside this, we also participate in discussions about image-making and visual storytelling, exploring how sequencing, framing, and narrative can influence the way a project is read. Through these combined exercises, the course helps us develop both technical skills and a clearer sense of authorship in how we present our work.

Environments I

Original ↗
Semester
Fall 2025
Instructors
Karel Klein
Angelica Lorenzi
Mira Henry
Jackilin Bloom
Aminatou Fall

In this design studio, we explore architecture through the relationship between interior and exterior space, focusing on how schools can be rethought under changing urban and environmental conditions. As students, we work through a series of projects that build on each other. The first project is a material “sandwich,” where we use found materials to stack and assemble layers, helping us understand spatial relationships and material logic in a hands-on way. The second project is a precedent study of the Centro Direzionale Subway Station in Naples, where we analyze drawings and reconstruct the project as a 3D model to better understand its spatial and structural system. In the final project, we combine what we learned from the first two exercises to design an elementary school in Los Angeles, using site observations to organize the building as an open, landscape-like system. Through this process, we begin to see how layering, material systems, and spatial organization can shape new types of learning environments.

Precident Study-Centro Direzionale Subway Station

The Centro Direzionale Subway Station in Naples, designed by Miralles Tagliabue EMBT and completed in 2025, is both a transport hub and an urban intervention. Covering about 10,000 (107,639 ft²)square meters, the station redefines the relationship between infrastructure and public space. Above ground, the project creates a new civic plaza with soft topographies, green areas, and ramps that ease the transition between different levels, turning what could have been a purely technical structure into a lively public realm. The roof is one of its most striking elements: a series of vaulted glulam timber arches that recall traditional railway stations while at the same time evoking natural, organic rhythms. Skylights are cut into these vaults, filtering daylight down into the underground concourses. Below, the walls and ceilings are clad with metallic finishes in Pompeii-inspired tones, referencing Naples’ layered cultural history. The station’s structure combines lightweight wooden vaults with the stability of concrete and steel, a dialogue between old and new materials. Together, the roof, ground, and structure create a fluid, integrated environment where circulation, light, and atmosphere blend seamlessly, transforming a metro stop into a civic landmark that feels both contemporary and deeply rooted in place.

Leubinger Station / Geometry & Infrastructure

Original ↗

The initial phase focused on isolating the primary geometries of the Leubinger station. By stripping away texture and color, the model highlights the relationship between the long horizontal canopy and the angular service block. These skeletal forms allow for a pure analysis of the building’s massing and structural logic before any environmental or contextual factors are introduced.

This section documents our detailed investigation into the station’s internal infrastructure. The 3D model translates specific site data, including the precise placement of fuel pumps, service zones, and internal room configurations, into a cohesive spatial logic. By mapping these functional components, we transition from a hollow shell to a fully articulated interior.

The exterior of Villa Savoye is perfect white concrete, from a distance this building looks like a perfect white modern ship floating on green grass. Its façade looks seamless from the whole and exudes a feeling of near perfection, a feeling that gives a sense of timelessness and avant-garde purity that doesn’t feel like a building made in that era. The villa exemplifies Le Corbusier’s “Five Points of Architecture,” including pilotis, a flat roof terrace, ribbon windows, an open plan, and a free façade, making it a masterful display of modernist principles (Kroll). The entire villa is like a white box suspended from slender pillars. This bold design approach is more of an exquisite sculpture than a house.

Approaching this building from the garden, one will not feel the boundary between natural and man- made objects. The building has an appearance that does not fit in with the surrounding landscape and environment, but its innovative large glass and levitation theme does not disturb the visual balance of the surroundings; it is more like levitating above the surroundings, and the innovative large windows give the building warmth. The green grass seems to separate the building from the woods outside, revealing a dreamy living machine, indifferent and unassuming.

The structure of the Villa Savoye appears to be unaffected by gravity. The elevated structure of the ground floor gives the impression that the subject of the house is suspended in the air, a design that allows circulation between air and space. The columns are very thin and stand like delicate reeds, elegant and firm, allowing the plane of the house to be unrestricted by the ground, visually weakening the abruptness that the columns bring to the building itself. This suspension of solidity gives the villa a weightless visual experience, as if it were not built but suspended.

In the interior of the villa, Corbusier still utilizes a minimalist style that matches the exterior. The spiral ramp, a key feature, guides visitors from the lower level to the open-air roof terrace. This design choice, in keeping with Corbusier’s concept of an “architectural promenade,” slows the movement of inhabitants and offers a dynamic spatial experience (Kroll). From the inside, Villa Savoye is a simple assemblage of planes and lines, a large white box suspended on a few very thin columns. Keyword: Gateway

The word “gateway” perfectly describes the architecture of the Grande Arche and the nature it gives to La Défense in Paris. The Grande Arche is an interpretation of the classical arch and a modern reinterpretation of the Arc de Triomphe in the central area of Paris. The Grande Arche has been a striking landmark since its construction, and from the historic center of Paris towards the modern business district of La Défense it represents the entrance to the new city as a threshold. I chose “gateway” as a keyword because it represents the building as a gateway, which can function as a large entrance, but also connects the past and the future of the city in both directions, whether it be from the old city to the new city or in the opposite direction, symbolizing the crossing. In this exploration I will do so by comparing the Grande Arche to its historical precedents. With the Grande Arche’s shaped design of the entrance and the collection of very bold shapes standing alone, architect Johann Otto wanted to create a sense of openness and transparency in the viewer’s senses; it is reminiscent of the Arc de Triomphe, to which it corresponds, along with the taller buildings, but the exaggerated and narrower blocks of white rectangles also reflect a sense of modernity. It serves as a symbolic link between two different regions and eras in the same city; it emphasizes not only the visual and spatial qualities of the building, but also the narratives embedded in it - narratives of transition, continuity and new beginnings.

Precedent 1: Siting & Plan; The Arch of Titus, Rome

The Arch of Titus functioned as one of the earliest and clearest architectural gateways in Roman urbanism; its position signaled entry into the sacred core of the Forum and marked the culmination of imperial processions. It precisely aligned with the Via Sacra, acting as a ceremonial threshold into the Roman Forum. It not only marked the end of military processions, but also visually connected key parts of the city through its axial alignment. The space surrounding the arch encouraged gathering, reinforcing its public and performative function. Its scale, central passage, and placement made it a hinge between everyday civic life and the sacred heart of Rome. The monument’s integration into the processional route gave it temporal and spatial meaning, it was a gate into a narrative space. There is a similarity in the exploration of the location of the Arc de Titus and the Grande Arche, both are landmarks in the central area of ​the city, the “gateway,” which gives a sense of power and space.

Precedent 2: Form; The Arc de Triomphe, Paris

The Arc de Triomphe is a monumental gateway and landmark that defines the axial of western Paris; it functions not only as a memorial but also as a spatial marker that frames the city’s ceremonial spine. It dominates its site with symmetry and weight. Its single arch frames space with dramatic clarity, and the dense sculptural reliefs on its surfaces project symbolic authority. The proportions are deliberate, scaled to the surrounding urban fabric yet clearly meant to be read from a distance. Pedestrians and vehicles move around and under it, reinforcing its identity as both monument and threshold. Its form does not simply contain space, it stages it, making the passage through feel ceremonial. This form of dialogue between past and present emphasizes a new entrance to the city and its cultural expression.

Image 5: Grande Arche Contextualization

Located in the La Défense business district, with Paris’ most modern skyline, the Grande Arche forms the backbone of the area. Much like the Brandenburg Gate in downtown Berlin, it is not only a central point, but also a symbol of historical change and a spatial and physical connection point that organizes the surroundings by defining boundaries and opening up views. In a deliberate alignment with the Arc de Triomphe and historic Paris, it translates visual and symbolic meanings from distance to time along a central axis; stretching through the centuries. The building is surrounded by tall office buildings, but its hollow shape is a unique urban landmark from the front and side. But its modern form blends into the background and does not appear to be intrusive. The surrounding large squares and crossroads create intersections for the building; highlighting centrality. It does not compete with the environment, but reshapes it. As a gateway and a new landmark for the city, it not only divides space, but also connects history and architectural language in a contextualized way, redefining environmental integration as an active gateway.

Conclusion:

The gramde arche explores how buildings can serve more than just sheltering functions, they can shape how we experience culture, history, and social change. Louis Kahn’s essay Monumentality reflects this idea by defining monuments as structures that embody permanence and stir collective memory. For Kahn, a true monument must go beyond appearance—it should emotionally resonate and mark something larger than its practical use. In contrast, Adrian Forty’s Words and Buildings approaches the idea

of “threshold” as a spatial condition: a moment of transition, a link between inside and outside, or between civic and personal life. For Forty, thresholds are not just doors or passages—they’re zones of interaction that structure everyday experience. Though coming from different angles, both writers touch on a shared idea: architecture can anchor people in time and space, helping them locate themselves socially and historically while acting as meaningful urban landmarks.

My case study, the Grande Arche in La Défense, Paris, captures this dual idea of monumentality and threshold. Positioned at the western end of Paris’s historical axis, its open cube form is both monumental in scale and porous in experience. Like the Arch of Titus, it aligns with the city’s major axis and frames civic movement. Its form recalls the Arc de Triomphe, but instead of stone and sculpted relief, it uses steel and glass, presenting an abstract, modern reimagining of a national monument. The Grande Arche opens space rather than enclosing it—it invites circulation, both physically and symbolically. The visible steel framework and minimalist detailing contrast with the richly carved surfaces of structures like the Arch of Septimius Severus, foregrounding a new kind of monument; one of openness and flow. Through these gestures, the Grande Arche doesn’t just echo history, it repositions it within a modern urban context. What makes the Grande Arche powerful is how it expresses the threshold not only through form, but through its integration with the city. It challenges the traditional notion of monumentality. Instead of anchoring itself through heavy materiality or ornate symbolism, it uses flatness, transparency, and modern construction to suggest a future-facing vision. This calls into question Kahn’s idea of timeless permanence, proposing instead that monuments can be light, flexible, and inviting. At the same time, it expands on Forty’s idea of the threshold, turning it into a large-scale urban experience. It becomes more than an object—it becomes a connector of past and future, of tradition and innovation. In doing so, the Grande Arche reframes how we think of monuments today. It proves that monumentality and threshold are not only compatible; they can be combined into a single architectural act that redefines how we encounter space, memory, and progress in the modern city.

not only through form, but through its integration with the city. It chal­

through heavy materiality or ornate symbolism, it uses flatness, transpar­
ency, and modern construction to suggest a future-facing vision. This calls 
into question Kahn’s idea of timeless permanence, proposing instead that 
monuments can be light, flexible, and inviting. At the same time, it ex­
pands on Forty’s idea of the threshold, turning it into a large-scale urban 
experience. It becomes more than an object—it becomes a connector of 
past and future, of tradition and innovation. In doing so, the Grande Arche 
reframes how we think of monuments today. It proves that monumentality 
and threshold are not only compatible; they can be combined into a sin­

I came to SCI-Arc with an interest in architecture, but not a clear idea of what it could be beyond finished buildings. Living in Downtown Los Angeles started to shift that. The city’s density, infrastructure, and constant movement made me more aware of how space is shaped by use, circulation, and change, rather than fixed form.

In the studio, I began to understand architecture as a process. I moved between drawing, modeling, and making, using both digital and physical tools to test ideas. Some projects were abstract, translating images into 3D forms or using simple operations to generate spatial models, while others focused on site and program, like an elementary school or a public pool. Working between these approaches made the process feel iterative rather than linear.

Material and environmental thinking also became important. Through fabrication, I worked with papier-mâché and casting, eventually building a full-scale chair. In another studio, I mapped flora and fauna and used that to develop a fitness center that integrates wind energy. These projects pushed me to think about architecture as part of larger environmental systems. At the same time, I explored how to represent work through photography and layout, using tools like robotic cameras to capture models under different lighting. Looking back, these experiences are less about final outcomes and more about how I think and work.

In the future, I want to continue working this way, developing projects that connect environmental systems, material experimentation, and spatial design.
